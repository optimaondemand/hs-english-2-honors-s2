The shippable standard
What finished means
●
A shippable game is one you could hand to a stranger today. Components are cut, labeled, and consistent. The rules match the game exactly. The look comes from your period research, not from a default style. Money does not decide this. A careful hand-made print-and-play game meets the standard. A generic or sloppy one does not, however expensive.
Tabletop (default): a print-and-play PDF is the floor. Optional pieces such as dice, tokens, or a 3D-print file are extras, never requirements. Digital (opt-in): a Twine story, a Tabletopia or Tabletop Simulator build, or a small app you design and prompt. Digital builds are graded on the design. If AI wrote code you cannot read or verify, log that honestly in your AI-Use Log. It caps the polish credit and leaves the design credit alone.
Where is your game right now: concept, prototype, or shippable? Give two reasons that name specific components or rules.
2
The final pass
Components, rules, and one fresh reader
●
Do one complete pass through every part of the game. Photograph or screenshot the finished game when you finish: all components laid out, plus one close-up of the piece you are proudest of.
List every component or screen, with its status: finished, needs work, or cut.
Pick three components. For each, name one choice you made for clarity or finish: spacing, wording, alignment, lettering, color.
Name two design choices that trace to a real period source from your research board, and the source for each.
A default fantasy, anime, or video-game look needs a period reason or a change.
Play one full round using only the Rules Document. List every place the rules and the game disagree, then fix the one that is wrong.
Ask someone who has not seen the final version to read the rules and set up the game. Record who, how long it took, and what they could not do without help.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
Your final rules say each player draws three cards. The finished deck is built for four cards per hand. What do you fix?
3
Revise the Statement
One comment, accepted or rejected with reasons
●
Your Statement draft from the last lesson now gets its revision. Get one comment from a peer or your teacher. Decide what to accept and what to reject, with reasons, then revise.
Paste or summarize one real comment on your Statement draft, and say who gave it.
What did you accept, and what exactly did you change in the Statement?
What did you reject, and what is your reason?
Paste the paragraph you improved most. Then say in one sentence what makes it stronger.
Check every in-text citation against your Works Cited, and every Works Cited entry against the text. Write what you corrected.
4
AI polish, verified
Accuracy and your own voice
●
AI can suggest layout fixes, wording, and clearer labels. It cannot tell you whether your game is fun or fair, and it can change your meaning while polishing. Verify every change for accuracy and for your own voice before you keep it.
What part of the game or the Statement did you ask AI to polish?
List each suggestion you kept, and each one you changed back or rejected. Give the reason.
Did any suggestion change what you meant or what a rule says? Show one example and your correction, or say how you checked.
