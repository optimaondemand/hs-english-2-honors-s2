The Defense and the rubric
Six criteria and one gate
●
The Designer's Defense is scored with the portfolio rubric below, the same rubric for tabletop and digital games. It grades design, craftsmanship, research, and argument. It does not grade production budget.
25%
Thematic embodiment
The mechanics, not just the setting, dramatize the theme. The game makes a coherent, defensible argument about its human question.
15%
Craftsmanship and aesthetic execution
Genuinely finished, made with evident care, with a researched visual and sonic identity. Judged on care and design thinking, not on money or coding skill.
15%
Playability and rules clarity
A non-designer can play to a conclusion from the materials alone. Rules are complete, unambiguous, and consistent.
15%
World grounding and research integration
The world is researched and credible. JStor scholarship visibly shapes the game, and departures from history are deliberate and explained.
15%
Written craft
The lore, the Designer's Analysis, and the Designer's Statement meet the quarter's writing standard with honors sophistication.
15%
Iteration and playtesting
Three documented cycles with meaningful, theme-serving revisions, not cosmetic tweaks.
The AI-Use Log is a gate. An honest, complete log showing verification and critique for every substantive AI use is required before the portfolio can be assessed at all. AI does not replace interpretation, design judgment, or your own skill.
Name the criterion where your portfolio is strongest and the one where it is weakest. Give a reason for each.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
Your game is a hand-cut print-and-play set with a researched look. A classmate's game is an expensive printed box in a generic fantasy style. How does the rubric treat them?

Mock Q&A
Answer the AI, then grade its questions
●
Give an AI tool your Statement and your game's rules. Ask it for eight questions a skeptical panel might ask in a defense. Answer three of them in writing. Then grade the AI's questions: which were sharp, which were shallow, and which misread your game.
Paste the AI's eight questions
Choose a question and write your answer in 80 to 120 words. Cite a rule or a playtest moment.
Choose a second question and answer it the same way.
Choose a third question and answer it the same way.
Grade the AI's questions. Name one that was sharp and why, one that was shallow, and one that misread your game, and point to the rule that already answers it.
●
Your AI-Use Log keeps one row for each time you use AI on this project. It is a requirement for your portfolio, and an incomplete log means the portfolio cannot be assessed. Write this lesson's row here, then copy it into your running log.

Record your Defense and a play session
Two recordings, saved and shared
●
The Defense has two recordings. Use any phone, laptop, or tablet. Save each file in OneDrive and share a view link. Record in a quiet room and check the sound before you begin.
The oral defense (6 to 8 minutes). Present your deck using the five-part arc, then answer two questions aloud: one about your rules, one about your sources. Speak to the camera, keep a steady pace, and pause after key points.

The recorded play session (5 to 10 minutes). Record a person who is not you playing your game from your materials, with you silent. If no one can play with you, record yourself playing every role and say so at the start. Show the moment you chose in your deck.
Where is your defense recording saved, and what is the share link or file name?
Where is your play recording saved, and who played? Name the moment of play that your deck points to.
Play each recording back. Write what you checked: the sound, the time, the moment you meant to show.

Assemble the Designer's Portfolio
Nine pieces and one gate
●
The Designer's Portfolio holds nine pieces. Gather them into one OneDrive folder named LastName_FirstName_Portfolio_ELA10H.
  1. The playable game. The final build from Lesson 16: print-and-play files or a digital link, with photos.
  2. The Rules Document. The final version, matched to the finished game.
  3. The world and lore document. Your setting and story, grounded in researched history (Lesson 2, revised).
  4. The Designer's Analysis. Your design rationale from Lesson 8, revised.
  5. The Designer's Statement. The final version from Lessons 15 and 16, with a Works Cited.
  6. Three playtest logs and a revision history. The logs from Lessons 7, 11, and 14, plus a list of what changed after each.
  7. The research log. Annotated JStor finds #1 to #4, ranked for reliability, with your research board.
  8. The AI-Use Log. Every row from every lesson, in one table.
  9. The year-end reflection. Written in the next tab.
AI-Use Log audit. The log is a gate. Before you submit, count the rows, check that every row shows how you verified or critiqued the AI's output, and fix any gap.
How many rows does your AI-Use Log have, and for which sprints?
List every row that was missing a verification or critique, and write what you added. If you found none, say how you checked.
Name one place where AI gave you something wrong or weak, and what you did about it. Name any part of a digital build that AI wrote and you cannot fully read.
List each of the nine pieces with its file name or location, and mark any piece that is still missing.
5
The year-end reflection
Four parts, specific moments
●
The year-end reflection is the last piece of the portfolio. Write 300 to 450 words in four parts. Use specific moments from your game and your logs.
Compare the question you started with to the claim your game makes now. What changed, and what caused it?
Name the playtest moment that changed your game most, and what you learned from it.
What did building the game teach you about how a person should govern themselves, and about what a community owes its members?
Where did AI help you think, and where did you have to overrule it? What will you do the same way, and differently, next time?
