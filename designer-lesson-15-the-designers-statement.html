What the Statement argues
A claim about the game, proved by its rules
●
The Designer's Statement is a researched argument about your game: how its rules make a claim about how to live well, alone and together. Your evidence is your own rules, what your testers did, and the scholarship you found. The Statement is not a literary essay on The Great Gatsby. The novel is one lens you used to test your win condition. Your subject is the game.
A MODEL OF THE MOVE, ON A DIFFERENT GAME
Weak: “My game shows that money cannot buy happiness, like the novel.” Strong: “Because every coin a player keeps is a coin the next player cannot use, the rules make saving a quiet act against the table. In playtest two, the player who saved most finished first and lost the others’ goodwill, which is the cost the game exists to show.”

Scholarship and your research log
Four finds, ranked and ready
●
Four thinkers are the common ground for games scholarship. Use any of them to sharpen your claim: borrow an idea, apply it to a rule of your game, or push back with evidence. Describe an idea only as far as you have read it.
JOHAN HUIZINGA, HOMO LUDENS
Argues that play is older than culture and helps to build it. Play happens in a space set apart from ordinary life, with its own rules, often called the magic circle.
ROGER CAILLOIS, MAN, PLAY AND GAMES
Sorts games by what they ask of players: competition, chance, role-play and simulation, and vertigo. Also separates free play from play bound by strict rules.
IAN BOGOST, PERSUASIVE GAMES
Argues that games can make claims through what their rules allow, reward, and prevent, and not only through their pictures and stories.
BERNARD DE KOVEN, THE WELL-PLAYED GAME
Asks what makes play good, fair, and worth doing, and treats the care players show one another as part of the game itself.
Find your sources on JStor. Go to jstor.org, choose to log in, and enter the school's shared username and password from the JStor tutorial in Module 1. You do not need a personal account. Search by the thinker's name, then by the idea. If a book is not on JStor, look for an article that discusses it, and cite what you actually read.

Plan the argument
One thesis, two rules, real evidence
●
Plan before you draft. A Statement of 1,000 to 1,400 words can hold one claim, two rules as evidence, one playtest moment each, a conversation with scholarship, and an answer to the strongest objection. More than that and it becomes a list.
Write your thesis: how your game's rules make an argument about how to live well, alone and together.
Name one rule in the sentence.
Rule 1: quote or describe it. What does it push players to do, and at what cost?
Rule 2: quote or describe it. What does it push players to do, and at what cost?
For each rule, one moment from a playtest log that shows a real player responding to it.
Which thinker or source will you use, and what will you do with the idea: apply it, extend it, or push against it?
A fair reader says your game actually endorses the opposite value. State that objection at its strongest.
If you worked through the hollow-value challenge in Lesson 13, begin there.
In two sentences: what does your game say about how a person should govern themselves, and what a community owes its members?
4
Draft the Statement
Six parts, then assemble in Word
●
Draft the six parts here, then assemble them in Word and add the MLA in-text citations and the Works Cited. Cite a source in the sentence that uses it: the author's last name and the page, in parentheses, as in (Lastname 42). Keep the draft between 1,000 and 1,400 words.
Part 1: the thesis paragraph (about 150 words)
Open with the claim and the question your game asks. Name the world. Do not retell the novel.
Part 2: the rules as the argument (about 250 words)
Walk through your two rules. For each, say what players are pushed to do and what it costs.
Part 3: the playtests (about 200 words)
Use specific moments from your logs. Say what a real player did and what it shows about the rule.
Part 4: the world and the scholarship (about 250 words)
Show how period research shaped a design choice, and how a game-studies source sharpens or challenges your claim. Cite in MLA.
Part 5: the strongest objection (about 200 words)
State it fairly, then answer it with a rule, a playtest moment, or a revision you made.
Part 6: alone and together (about 150 words)
Close on what the game says about self-government and about what a community owes its members.
Works Cited: list every source you cite, in MLA format, in alphabetical order.

AI feedback on logic and sources
Then decide for yourself
●
Give an AI tool your Statement draft. Ask it to check the logic of your argument and the quality of your sources. AI can point to gaps in reasoning. It cannot confirm that a source exists, and it sometimes names articles that do not exist. Check every source it mentions on JStor. Keep your own voice: you decide which suggestions to accept.
